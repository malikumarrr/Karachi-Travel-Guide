The logic behind Compass

Every rule in this prototype is lifted from Caprae’s own training sessions — none of it is invented:

The multiples cheat-sheet. Under $1M in owner earnings → 3–5×. $1–5M → 4–8×. Above $5M → 6–10×. Software → 3–6× ARR. Premiums when net dollar retention clears 110% and no customer exceeds ~15% of revenue.

The plays. The “I don’t need you” flip (war chest, buy out your competitors), the succession conversation (“tell me about your son” — never a yes/no), the co-founder-you-never-had framing, the time-to-close pain for sellers burned by slow buyers.

The guardrails. Never open with a yes/no question. Never say “incubator” — say “sponsored search.” Never say “round robin” or “right of first refusal” on a call. Never cite the owner’s age — cite their tenure.

Why prompts, not scripts. The most repeated critique in Caprae’s own call reviews is sounding scripted. Compass therefore surfaces cues and options — it never writes the caller’s lines for them. It surfaces up to three cues at once — each with a few ways to say it and the named tactic behind it — so the caller has options without being buried.

Boundaries. Everything here is an indicative, pre-NDA anchoring aid. It never replaces the real path: mutual NDA → three years of financials → data room → a proper assessment.

Why this panel exists

From Caprae’s own playbook: after a buyer passes on a deal, the seller gets “a second assessment… optimistic, neutral, pessimistic — no one does this in the PE world.” This tab productises that assessment: normalise the earnings, screen the quality, price the fixes, and show the owner three honest futures. It is the analyst’s half of Compass — the call co-pilot wins the conversation; this wins the mandate.
